# Narrative copy strategy — how to write posts that flow (Joe, June 2026)

This is the craft layer that sits ON TOP of the #1 process rule
("process source into UNDERSTANDING, then write FROM it"). The process rule
governs WHERE the words come from; this file governs HOW they're shaped.

> ⚠️ This file is about POST COPY (headlines/captions). The UNDERSTANDING doc
> itself — the digested ideas Joe reads and approves before posts — has its OWN
> stricter bar: plain-language, glanceable, no clever labels. See
> `references/plain-language-rules.md` (Joe corrected this HARD on SI Ep031).
> Don't confuse the two: the understanding must be instantly clear to a human;
> the post copy uses narrative mechanics. Both ban clever-but-vague phrasing.

## ⛔ NARRATIVE PRINCIPLES ARE A TOOLKIT TO CHOOSE FROM — NOT a story mold poured into every post (Joe corrected this HARD, SI Ep031, June 2026)
This is the most-repeated failure of the Ep031 session. "Use narrative
principles" does NOT mean "write a little story every time." I staged the SAME
scene-with-a-protagonist (a guy in a truck in his driveway, the phone ringing)
across LinkedIn + IG + X + FB — so every post sounded identical. Joe: *"I keep
telling you not to be literal… you just literally created stories for the posts.
I said use the narrative principles to create [pull], not stories every time."*

The levers below are a TOOLKIT. A post may use ONE of them, or none-as-a-scene:
- a blunt one-liner carried by a real NUMBER,
- a contrarian REFRAME ("A missed call doesn't cost you a call. It costs you a
  customer — and you never find out"),
- a direct QUESTION hook,
- a plain observation that reframes itself.
The story-SCENE (protagonist + specific moment unfolding) is just ONE tool. Use
it SPARINGLY — at most once in a batch, and usually in the LONGER slot
(Facebook/LinkedIn) where it has room to breathe. Never make it the default for
all four platforms. Range across the batch is the goal; four scenes in a row is
the opposite of range. Decide per-post: "which ONE mechanic fits this platform
and this angle?" — not "how do I tell this as a story?"

## What "narrative strategy" means (Joe's definition, confirmed)
NOT telling a literal story start-to-finish. It's using the *mechanics* of
storytelling to lower the reader's mental effort so they glide through the post
and keep reading (cognitive fluency). The levers (a toolkit — see the rule
above; pick what fits, don't apply all every time):
- **Open loop** — line 1 starts a question the brain needs closed, so it keeps
  reading (Zeigarnik effect). Don't telegraph the payoff in the headline.
- **One concrete protagonist + one specific moment** — "a squeaky $500 chair,"
  not "email marketing." Specific = visual = effortless to process.
- **Tension → turn → payoff** — a small problem, a shift, a result. The shape of
  a story without being a fairy tale.
- **Cognitive fluency** — short lines, ONE idea per line, plain words, real
  cadence. Each sentence pulls the next.

## Brand voice is NOT the problem — execution is
Joe confirmed the SI voice (direct, contrarian, practitioner, self-aware, dry
wit, no hype) is right. The failure was treating "short and punchy" as license
for choppy fragments. SI voice is short **with rhythm**: full sentences, real
cadence, ONE clean open loop, no juvenile slang. Only fall back to Joe's
personal voice if a story angle forces warmth/teaching that breaks SI's edge —
and that is rare; most SI angles stay in SI voice.

## The single biggest mistake (do not repeat)
**Lifting/paraphrasing transcript phrases into headlines and captions.** Spoken
words were never built to be a headline; dropped in raw they read cheap and
choppy — Joe's words: "like a three-year-old wrote it." Plausible-but-clever
abstractions are just as bad because they add friction (the reader stops to
decode), which is the opposite of the narrative goal.

### Rejected vs. accepted (real examples from SI Ep030 "cappuccino" angle)
- ❌ "He built it to save time. The time did something else." — vague; *time*
  doesn't *do* anything; reader stalls on "what does that mean?"
- ❌ "He didn't expect what the time did to him." — same abstraction, nonsensical.
- ❌ "He automated one email to save time. He got his brain back instead." — the
  first line is good, but "got his brain back" is forced transcript-slang glued
  on as a choppy fragment; telegraphs the twist and kills curiosity.
- ✅ The fix that worked = keep the good concrete setup line, then write the real
  CONSEQUENCE in full-sentence cadence, letting the body deliver the turn:
  > Peter automated one daily email to save time.
  > He didn't expect to get his brain back. *(opens the loop — back from what?)*
  …then the body pays it off with the concrete cappuccino moment and the new
  product idea that arrived in the quiet.

### A rejected line can become OK when it IS the turn, not glued to one (SI Ep030 idea 5, June 2026)
"He got his brain back" was rejected above as forced transcript-slang *because it was glued onto a setup line as a choppy second fragment*. But the SAME phrase works when it stands as the headline's own clean turn — e.g. an X card whose entire headline is "He set out to save time. / He got his brain back instead." Here the headline IS the tension→turn (two full-cadence clauses), and "brain back" is the concrete payoff with the BODY delivering the quiet (the cappuccino, the new idea). Lesson: a phrase isn't banned in the absolute — it's banned as a tacked-on fragment. As the deliberate turn line, in full-sentence cadence, with the body paying it off, it lands. Judge the phrase by its STRUCTURAL role, not a blacklist.

### The intuitive move that fixes itWhen you have a strong setup line, do NOT ask "what words come next in the
transcript." Ask: **"What did this actually DO — the real consequence — beyond
the obvious?"** Then write THAT in brand voice. (Ep030: the automation didn't
just save 3 hours; the quiet it created is where a new product idea arrived =
the real payoff was headspace, not time.)

### NAME THE MECHANISM — don't gesture at it with a container metaphor (Joe corrected this directly, SI Ep030 idea 5, June 2026)
Doing the "what did it actually DO?" step is necessary but NOT sufficient. This
session I did that step and still landed on **vague container metaphors** for
the payoff — and Joe rejected every one of them in turn:
- ❌ "There's just no room for them to land" — Joe: *"Saying there is no room for
  them to land is vague."* (Where? room made of what? reader has to fill it in.)
- ❌ "your headspace" / ❌ "the quiet is where the next idea shows up" /
  ❌ "room to think" — all the SAME miss: a spatial/atmospheric stand-in for the
  real thing instead of the real thing.

**The rule:** when you've found the real consequence, push ONE level further and
name the literal MECHANISM that produced it — the noun the reader can't
reinterpret. For Ep030 that mechanism is **attention**: a recurring task is an
open loop, every open loop charges rent on your attention, and an idea needs
free attention to form. So the payoff isn't "he got headspace/quiet/room" — it's
**"he got his attention back; a mind tracking forty open loops has nothing left
to build with."** Same insight, but now it's mechanical and concrete, not a mood.

Test before shipping a payoff line: *could the reader read this and still not
know exactly what changed?* If "headspace / room / space / the quiet / clarity /
mental load" could be swapped for each other without loss, you've written the
container, not the mechanism. Name the mechanism.

(Note: this supersedes the earlier "brain back can work as the turn" rehab below
for THIS angle — Joe ultimately wanted the attention mechanism named, not any
"brain back / headspace" framing. The structural lesson there still holds in
general; the content lesson is: prefer the named mechanism over any metaphor.)

## When a raw line CAN be quoted verbatimOnly if it's strong enough to stand alone, e.g. "There's no such thing as spam —
just shitty emails." / "Don't teach it. Do it with it." If paraphrasing a line
cheapens it, don't paraphrase — write fresh from the insight. Never manufacture
a detail to make a line land.

## Headline option patterns (pick the rhythm, all full-sentence)
- **Understated:** "The work was done. So he just sat there."
- **Contrarian reframe:** "Everyone wants the three hours back. He wanted the silence."
- **The turn (sets up payoff w/o spoiling):** "He set out to save time. What he found was harder to buy."
